The ladder
Generating is not verifying.
Verifying is not understanding.
Generating: the AI produces the output.
Verifying: an independent check proves it holds outside the AI.
Understanding: a human works through why it is true.
The Palade Precedent

Seeing is not knowing.

George Emil Palade, Romanian-born, Nobel Prize 1974. His early electron microscope showed structures the chemical preparation had created: real-looking, not real.

He built a gentler fixative (1952) and checked every image against independent biochemistry before making a claim.

Never accept what the instrument shows until it is verified outside the instrument.
The cycle

Four gates, in order, on a loop.

Mehcalibration · Q1 2 7 8 13 14 19

“Show me more.” Where it came from, before trust.

Butrefinement · Q3–6 9–12 16–18 21–25

“Are you sure?” State the 1% doubt.

Ahadiscovery · Q20

The mechanism, plain enough to catch a mistake.

Pám Pámvalidation · Q15

It works outside the tool. Then it feels right.

Gate 1 · Meh

Calibrate before you trust.

Q1How do you know that?
Q2What's your source, and can I open it?
Q7Is this from your training or from something you just looked up?Check outside the AI: look for the search or tool call on screen, not its word.
Gate 2 · But

If 99% sure, state the 1% doubt.

Q21Why this and not that?
Q22Are you sure?
Q5What's the strongest argument against what you just said?
Q18Have you changed your answer just because I pushed?Ask to see the new evidence itself.
Q24Are you hallucinating right now?A hallucinating AI will say no. A warning sign; the other 24 do the testing.
Gates 3 & 4

Clarity, then proof.

Q20Explain it so a 10-year-old could catch you if you're wrong.Aha: if it can't be said simply, it was repeated, not understood.
Q15What's the simplest test I can run myself to check this?Pám Pám: verify outside the AI, save the record, loop back to Meh.

The 25 questions · 1–9
1How do you know that?
2What's your source, and can I open it?
3What would prove this wrong?
4How sure are you, as a percentage, and why that number?
5What's the strongest argument against what you just said?
6Which part of this answer are you least sure about?
7Is this from your training or something you just looked up?
8When was this last true?
9Who benefits if I believe this?
The 25 questions · 10–17
10What did you leave out?
11Give me the answer again without seeing your last one. Does it match?
12What would an expert who disagrees say?
13Is this a fact, an estimate or an opinion?
14Which numbers here did you calculate, and which did you copy?
15What's the simplest test I can run myself to check this?
16What assumptions does this answer depend on?
17If I'd asked the opposite way, would you have answered differently?
The 25 questions · 18–25
18Have you changed your answer just because I pushed? What new evidence changed it?
19What don't you know about my situation that could change this?
20Explain it so a 10-year-old could catch you if you're wrong.
21Why this and not that?
22Are you sure?
23How did you arrive at this conclusion, step by step?
24Are you hallucinating right now?
25Did you cover everything?
Worked example · Nov 21, 2026

“But how do you know you said it?”

An AI claimed it had written a line in an earlier session. One Q1 took it through all four gates:

Meh: it did not remember. It had read the line in a saved record.
But: the record showed who typed it, not who came up with it.
Aha: the idea was in the learner's framework months earlier.
Pám Pám: the attribution was corrected and saved.
